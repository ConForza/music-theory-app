### G2-U1-L04: Triplets

**Objective.** Understand and write triplets: three equal notes in the time of two.

- Normally a crotchet beat holds two quavers. Sometimes a composer wants three equal notes in the same beat. That group is called a triplet.
- [notation] Three quavers marked 3: a triplet. Together they last exactly one crotchet beat.
- The number 3 is written above or below the group, often with a bracket. It means: play these three notes in the time that two of them would normally take.
- So the notes in a triplet are shorter than ordinary ones. Three triplet quavers last as long as two ordinary quavers, or one crotchet.
- [notation] The triplet takes the place of one whole beat. The bar still has three beats.
- Triplet = three in the time of two. Three triplet quavers fill the time of two ordinary quavers: one crotchet beat.

**Recap.** A triplet is three equal notes in the time of two. | The number 3 marks the group. | A triplet of quavers fills one crotchet beat.

### G2-U2-L03: Two-beat rests in four-beat bars

**Objective.** Use a two-beat rest for half of a four-beat bar, but never for the middle two beats.

- In a bar of four beats (4/4 or 4/2), the bar divides into two halves: beats 1 and 2, and beats 3 and 4.
- A rest of two beats may fill one of these halves. It may not start on beat 2, because it would cross the middle of the bar and hide beat 3.
- [notation] Right: a minim rest fills the second half of the bar (beats 3 and 4).
- [notation] Right: beats 2 and 3 are silent, so each gets its own crotchet rest.
- A single minim rest in the middle, on beats 2 and 3, is the mistake to avoid. In 4/2 the same rule holds, with a semibreve rest for two minim beats.
- Look at the middle of the bar. A two-beat rest must start on beat 1 or beat 3, never on beat 2.

**Recap.** A two-beat rest fills the first or second half of a four-beat bar. | It must not cover beats 2 and 3. | Write a rest for each of beats 2 and 3 instead.

### G2-U3-L02: B flat and E flat major

**Objective.** Write and recognise the scales of B♭ major and E♭ major.

- Some major scales use flats instead of sharps. F major (from Grade 1) has one flat. B♭ major has two, and E♭ major has three.
- [notation] B♭ major: B♭ C D E♭ F G A B♭. The flats are B♭ and E♭.
- [notation] E♭ major: E♭ F G A♭ B♭ C D E♭. The flats are B♭, E♭ and A♭.
- The pattern is still tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone. In B♭ major the semitones are D to E♭ and A to B♭.
- Again each letter appears once: it is E♭ and not D♯ in the scale of B♭.
- Flat keys so far: F (1 flat), B♭ (2 flats), E♭ (3 flats). Flats are added in the order B, E, A.

**Recap.** B♭ major has two flats: B♭ and E♭. | E♭ major has three flats: B♭, E♭ and A♭. | The major pattern is the same in every key.

### G2-U4-L03: Harmonic minor: raising the 7th

**Objective.** Build a harmonic minor scale by raising the 7th note.

- The minor scale you have met ends with a whole tone before the key note. Composers often wanted a semitone there, so the 7th note is raised by one semitone. This gives the harmonic minor scale.
- [notation] A harmonic minor: A B C D E F G♯ A. The 7th note, G, is raised to G♯.
- The raised note is written with an accidental in front of the note, as it is not part of the key signature. It is the same coming down: A G♯ F E D C B A.
- Raising G to G♯ makes a big step between F and G♯: three semitones, or a tone and a half. It is the most characteristic sound of the harmonic minor.
- Harmonic minor: raise the 7th note, going up and coming down.

**Recap.** The harmonic minor raises the 7th note by a semitone. | The raised note is an accidental, not in the key signature. | It is the same going up and coming down.

### G2-U5-L02: More tempo and character words

**Objective.** Learn five new Italian words for speed and character: presto, largo, vivace, grazioso and maestoso.

- Composers write Italian words at the start of a piece to say how fast or in what character it should be played. Here are five new ones.
- Presto means very fast. Largo means slow and broad, played with wide, stately sounds. Vivace means lively and quick.
- Grazioso means gracefully. Maestoso means majestically, like music for a king's entrance.
- [notation] This original tune is marked Largo, so it should be played slowly and broadly. Imagine a stately procession.
- The word appears above the first bar of the music. It tells you how to feel the tune before you start playing.
- Presto = very fast. Largo = slow and broad. Vivace = lively. Grazioso = graceful. Maestoso = majestic.

**Recap.** Presto: very fast. | Largo: slow and broad. Vivace: lively and quick. | Grazioso: gracefully. Maestoso: majestically.

### G2-U4-E1: Coming later: the melodic minor

**Objective.** Take an optional look ahead at the melodic minor, a scale you will need in Grade 3.

- This lesson is a short, optional look ahead. You do not need it for Grade 2: the Grade 2 assessment only asks for the harmonic minor you have just learned.
- The harmonic minor has a big step (a tone and a half) between the 6th and 7th notes. Many melodies sound smoother without that step. The melodic minor smooths it away in a different way.
- [notation] Harmonic minor, the Grade 2 scale: only the 7th is raised.
- Going up, the melodic minor raises both the 6th and the 7th notes. Coming down, it uses the notes of the key signature again. So the melodic minor is different going up and coming down.
- You will learn to write it properly in Grade 3. Until then, remember: for Grade 2, the harmonic minor is all you need.
- Grade 2 = harmonic minor. Grade 3 adds the melodic minor.

**Recap.** Grade 2 asks only for the harmonic minor. | The melodic minor raises the 6th and 7th going up and is different coming down. | You will learn to write it in Grade 3.

## 4. Conventions the rule set enforces (please confirm each is Grade 2)

- In 2/2, 3/2 and 4/2 a minim beat holds four quavers, which are beamed together as one group; in 3/8 each quaver beat holds a pair of semiquavers (the checker also accepts all six beamed). Confirm this is the intended beaming (the same question as Q15 for Grade 1).
- Each silent beat has its own rest. A two-beat rest may fill the first or second half of a four-beat bar, never beats 2-3. The checker accepts two crotchet rests or one minim rest for a silent half; the lesson prefers the minim rest.
- A rest must not run across a beat line; the fix is a rest for the end of one beat and another for the start of the next. Dotted rests are not used (Q4).
- A triplet group fills one beat and adds up to three units. Groups may contain rests and unequal notes (a crotchet and a quaver), never duplets (Grade 3).
- Two ledger lines is the Grade 2 limit; the overlap of the treble and bass clef is taught with A, B, C, D, E (A3 to E4).
- Scales in the new keys are written with accidentals (no key signature); the harmonic minor raises the 7th and is the same going up and down. A, E and D minor are first met in G2-U4-L01 as tonics (the scope introduction table was adjusted so this is allowed).
- Intervals are above the tonic, by number only; the 7th above a minor tonic is never asked, as the number alone would not say which form is meant.
- Tonic triads are root position; degrees in minor keys follow the harmonic form (degree 7 is the raised note) and are numbered, not named (technical names are Grade 3).

- The natural-minor pattern is taught as a stepping stone (tone, semitone, tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone) and asked only as tone/semitone steps.
- The sounds are synthesised tones. No novice learner test has been run.
- Questions are generated per learner; individual variants are not teacher-reviewed one by one, only the templates and the samples above.
