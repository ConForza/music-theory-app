### G5-U1-L02: Grouping in irregular time

**Objective.** Group the notes and rests of an irregular bar so that they show the stated grouping.

- In an irregular bar each group is written as if it were a bar of its own: just as it would be if it were a complete group of two or three beats. There are no special rules beyond that. A group of three quavers is joined by one beam; a group of two is joined by another; the beams never join notes from two different groups.
- [notation] The same five quavers: 3 + 2 in the first bar, 2 + 3 in the second.
- A bar that adds up is not necessarily a bar that is grouped well. Check three things one at a time. First, the total: does the bar add up to the signature? Second, the grouping: do the groups match the pattern asked for? Third, the notation: are the beams and rests grouped inside each group?
- [notation] In 5/4 the groups are shown by note lengths: nothing crosses from beat 3 into beat 4.
- Rests follow the same idea. A whole group may be one rest, and a single beat may be one rest; a rest that begins in one group and ends in another is not used. Questions always say which grouping is wanted, so you never have to guess.
- Name the grouping first (for example 2 + 3), then fill or beam one group at a time, left to right.

**Recap.** Each group of two or three is written like a bar of its own. | A beam never joins notes from two groups; no note or rest crosses a group. | Check the total, the grouping and the beams separately.

### G5-U2-L03: Octave transposition between any clefs

**Objective.** Transpose a melody exactly one octave higher or lower using any of the four clefs, without confusing this with a change of clef at the same pitch.

- You may be asked to write a melody an octave higher or lower in another clef. There are three different things a copy can be. It can sound at exactly the SAME pitch (only the clef changed). It can be EXACTLY ONE OCTAVE higher or lower. Or it can be wrong: the note positions on the stave have simply been copied into a different clef, so every note has changed to a different, unrelated pitch.
- Two mistakes are very common. One is to use the wrong C clef: the alto instead of the tenor, or the other way round. The other is to write the melody at the wrong pitch: usually at the same pitch when an octave was asked, sometimes two octaves away. Read the question twice: which clef is wanted, and which way?
- [notation] A phrase in the tenor clef starting on middle C.
- [notation] One octave higher, in the treble clef: C D E F an octave above middle C.
- To transpose by an octave: move each note eight letters up or down (the same letter name, the next octave number), then write it in the clef named. The key signature stays the same, because an octave is the same note names.
- Same pitch: same name and octave number. One octave away: same name, octave number one higher or lower. Neither: copied positions.

**Recap.** A copy can be the same pitch, one octave away, or wrongly copied by position. | An octave: the same letter names, the octave number one more or one less. | Check which clef the question names.

### G5-U3-L06: Transposing for B flat, A and F instruments

**Objective.** Transpose a short melody between concert pitch and the written part for an instrument in B♭, A or F, with the right key signature and accidentals.

- To transpose a melody for one of these instruments, first find the new key. The question tells you which interval to use and whether to go up or down. A melody in D major, to be written for a clarinet in A (up a minor 3rd), becomes F major: D up a minor 3rd is F. Then write the new key signature, and then every note.
- [notation] At concert pitch, in D major.
- [notation] Written for a clarinet in A, up a minor 3rd: F major.
- Do not assume the accidentals stay the same. Every note moves by the same interval, so each note keeps its relation to the tonic. A G♯ in D major is an augmented 4th above the tonic; an augmented 4th above F is B natural, so the G♯ becomes B♮ in F major (not B♭).
- Do not respell a note as its enharmonic twin. B natural could be written C♭ and would sound the same, but C♭ is a diminished 5th above F, not an augmented 4th, so it would be wrong. Always move the letter names by the interval. Copy over any performance directions (tempo, dynamics, slurs) that the original has, as you would for a real performance. You may also be asked to check someone else's transposition: find the new key first and compare the key signature, then check each note against the interval, one at a time.
- New key first, then the key signature, then every note by the same interval. Concert to written goes up; written to concert goes down.

**Recap.** Find the new key first, then give the part its key signature. | Move every note by the same interval; adjust accidentals by the interval. | Never respell a note as its enharmonic twin.

### G5-U4-L04: Perfect, plagal and imperfect cadences

**Objective.** Name perfect, plagal and imperfect cadences and the chord pairs that make them.

- A cadence is a resting place at the end of a phrase, a little like punctuation in a sentence. Some cadences feel like a full stop, others like a comma. Each uses two different chords, one after the other. In Grade 5 you work with cadences in the major keys of C, G, D and F.
- Two cadences end on the tonic chord. The perfect cadence is the dominant chord followed by the tonic chord (V–I). The plagal cadence is the subdominant chord followed by the tonic chord (IV–I). Any cadence that ends on the dominant chord is an imperfect cadence. At this grade the chords before the dominant are I, ii or IV: I–V, ii–V or IV–V.
- [notation] Perfect cadence in C major: V–I.
- [notation] Plagal cadence in C major: IV–I.
- [notation] Imperfect cadence in C major: I–V, ending on the dominant.
- Perfect: V–I. Plagal: IV–I. Imperfect: anything that ends on V (I–V, ii–V or IV–V). Name the last two chords first.

**Recap.** Perfect: V–I. Plagal: IV–I. Imperfect: ends on V. | Imperfect cadences here: I–V, ii–V, IV–V. | Name the last two chords first.

### G5-U5-L07: Exploring a score

**Objective.** Read a short original score and answer connected questions about its key, clef, harmony, cadence, instruments, ornaments and markings.

- A score tells you many things at once. Work through it in the same order every time. First the clef and the key signature: which clef is it, and what key? Then the time signature and the markings at the start (a tempo or expression term). Then the instrument: what is it, how does it make its sound, and does it sound as written?
- Then look at the harmony. In a piece for an instrument and piano, the chords are in the piano part. Gather the notes of one bar, find the root, and the lowest note gives the position. The last two chords tell you the cadence: V–I perfect, IV–I plagal, or a pair ending on V imperfect.
- Last, look at the details on the notes: an ornament sign (trill, turn or mordent), or an interval between two marked notes, where you count letter names for the number and use the key for the quality. Each question points at something printed, so the answer is always in the score.
- [notation] A cello line in the tenor clef, marked teneramente: clef, key, time signature and direction all tell you something.
- Clef and key first; then the instrument and markings; then the chords and cadence; then the marked notes.

**Recap.** Clef and key signature first. | Instrument, tempo term and markings next. | Chords and cadence from the notes; each question points to something printed.

## 4. Conventions the rule set enforces (please confirm each is Grade 5)

- Irregular time at Grade 5 is 5/4, 7/4, 5/8 and 7/8 only, as groups of two and three, and occasionally four: 3+2 or 2+3 in five; 2+2+3, 3+2+2, 2+3+2, 4+3 or 3+4 in seven (questions draw only the groups of two and three; a learner's 4+3 or 3+4 is accepted). The signature does not give the grouping; a question that needs it always states it. In x/4 the grouping is shown by note lengths, in x/8 by beams (Q30, Q35).
- Irregular divisions: a quintuplet is five notes in the time of four, a sextuplet six in the time of four, a septuplet seven in the time of four, a nonuplet nine in the time of eight. Written values: semiquavers in the time of a crotchet (demisemiquavers for nine), demisemiquavers in the time of a quaver, semiquavers for nine in the time of a minim.
- Tenor clef: middle C on the fourth line, the lines D F A C E; used by cello, bassoon and tenor trombone. Octave transposition between any of the four clefs; the tenor clef is always involved in a question. A copy at the same pitch, a copy exactly one octave away and a copy of the stave positions are three different things and are graded as such.
- Six-accidental keys: F♯ major and D♯ minor (six sharps), G♭ major and E♭ minor (six flats); the harmonic form raises the seventh (C𝄪 in D♯ minor, D♮ in E♭ minor); scales are asked in the treble and bass clefs, each letter used once. A♭, D♭ and G♭ minor are not used (the book replaces them by their enharmonic equivalents).
- Intervals: a diatonic interval joins two notes of one major or minor scale; a chromatic interval joins two notes that share none. Number from the letters, quality from the semitones or the major scale of the lower note; never doubly augmented or diminished. A compound interval counts on past 8 (a 12th is a 5th plus an octave: 7 + 5); both 'major 10th' and 'compound major 3rd' are accepted.
- Transposing instruments: B♭ sounds a major 2nd lower than written, A a minor 3rd lower, F a perfect 5th lower. Concert to written goes up, written to concert goes down; the new key is found first, then every note is moved by the interval and respelt by the interval (never by its enharmonic equivalent).
- Chords: root position five-three (a), first inversion six-three (b), second inversion six-four (c); the lowest note alone decides; spacing and doubling never matter. Chords identified and written are I, ii, IV and V in the keys of the Grade, with minor-key chords from the harmonic minor; ii is diminished in a minor key. Letters (a, b, c) and figures are both accepted (Q14, Q33).
- Cadences: perfect V–I, plagal IV–I, imperfect: any cadence ending on V (I–V, ii–V, IV–V here). Only in C, G, D and F major. A melody question accepts any pair of chords that contains the melody notes and makes the named cadence.
- Ornaments: only replacing written-out notes with a sign is assessed (trill, turn, upper mordent, lower mordent). The acciaccatura is crushed and quick; the appoggiatura leans and takes half of an undotted main note and two thirds of a dotted one. Performance is described in words and never asked as notes to be written out.
- Voices: soprano, alto, tenor and bass are the four choral voices; mezzo-soprano lies between soprano and alto, baritone between tenor and bass. Only the two unmistakable ranges are asked (Q34).
- Instruments: piccolo (higher flute), cor anglais (deeper oboe), bass clarinet (deeper clarinet), double bassoon (deeper bassoon); single reed for the clarinets and saxophone, double reed for the oboe family and the bassoons, none for flute, piccolo and recorder; two tenor trombones and a bass trombone; definite and indefinite pitch percussion; the harp is a string instrument outside the string section; guitar, saxophone, recorder, harpsichord, clavichord and organ are not regular orchestra members; organ music uses three staves.
- Optional enrichment (never compulsory, never in any assessment): Ic–V, SATB in short score (reading the voices only). Approach chords are compulsory and assessed (taught in G5-U4-L06; G5-U4-E2 is extra practice).
