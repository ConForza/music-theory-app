### G3-U1-L03: Grouping notes in compound time

**Objective.** Group notes so that every beat of compound time can be seen.

- Good grouping lets a player see each beat at a glance. In compound time, each beat is a dotted crotchet, so every beat should be easy to spot.
- [notation] Beam the quavers of each beat together. Do not join groups from different beats.
- A note can be written as a single note only if it sits inside one beat, or fills whole beats from one beat line to another. A crotchet and a quaver in one beat are written separately, a crotchet then a quaver.
- [notation] Two whole beats are one dotted minim, not two dotted crotchets joined by a tie.
- When a long sound begins or ends in the middle of a beat, split it into two notes and join them with a tie. The tie shows where the beat line falls. Do not use a tie where a single note would do. Show every beat: beam in threes, use a dotted minim for two whole beats, and tie only where a note would hide the beat.
- [notation] A dotted crotchet tied to a quaver: the tie crosses into the second beat.

**Recap.** Beam the three quavers of each beat together; never across a beat. | A whole beat is a dotted crotchet; two whole beats are a dotted minim. | Tie a note that crosses a beat line so the beat stays visible.

### G3-U2-L02: Beaming very short notes

**Objective.** Group demisemiquavers (and the notes around them) so that every beat can be seen.

- Short notes are easiest to read when they are beamed in beats. The rule is the one you know from quavers and semiquavers: join the notes of one beat with a beam, and never join notes from two different beats.
- [notation] 2/4: one beam for each crotchet beat.
- In compound time the same rule applies to the dotted-crotchet beat: all the short notes of one beat share a beam, and a new beam starts on the next beat.
- [notation] 6/8: the beam covers exactly the first beat.
- A beam is a way of showing beats, so a beam that runs across a beat line is wrong, even if the notes add up. In this course a beat never has more than four demisemiquavers in a row, so every beat can be beamed with one beam. Demisemiquaver rests are arranged in the same way as semiquaver and quaver rests. You will meet them mixed with notes in later grades.
- One beam for each beat. Never beam across a beat line.

**Recap.** Beam the short notes of each beat together. | Start a new beam on each beat; never beam across a beat line. | The same rule works in simple and in compound time.

### G3-U3-L03: Transposing at the octave between treble and bass

**Objective.** Transpose a melody an octave up or down, into the treble or the bass clef.

- To transpose music is to write it higher or lower, so that it sounds higher or lower. In Grade 3 you transpose at the octave, and you may also change from the treble clef to the bass clef, or the other way round. Take middle C. In the treble clef it is on the first ledger line below the stave. The same pitch in the bass clef is on the first ledger line above the stave, as in the picture below.
- [notation] The same pitch as the treble clef's middle C: this is NOT an octave lower.
- [notation] An octave lower, in the bass clef: the second space.
- The last picture is the answer to “write this note an octave lower in the bass clef”. Two things have changed: the clef, and the octave. Writing the same pitch in the new clef would only change the picture, not the sound. To transpose a melody: choose the new clef and the direction. Move every note by one octave, which is eight letter names, seven lines and spaces. Keep the key signature, and write any accidentals the notes need. Then check the first and last notes. Octave transposition = new clef AND one octave up or down. Same pitch in a new clef is not a transposition.
- [notation] A melody in the treble clef, in G major.
- [notation] An octave lower in the bass clef. Same key signature, every note an octave down.

**Recap.** Transposing at the octave moves every note up or down eight letter names. | Changing the clef alone does not transpose: the pitch must move too. | Keep the key signature and write accidentals as needed.

### G3-U4-L01: The melodic minor

**Objective.** Learn the melodic minor scale, and how it differs from the harmonic minor.

- In Grade 2 you learned the harmonic minor: the notes of the key signature with the 7th raised by a semitone. It has an awkward big step between the 6th and 7th notes.
- [notation] Harmonic minor: only the 7th is raised, going up and coming down.
- The melodic minor smooths that step away. Going up, it raises both the 6th and the 7th notes. Coming down, it lowers them again, so the notes are those of the key signature. That means the melodic minor is different going up and coming down.
- [notation] A melodic minor going up: F and G are both raised.
- [notation] A melodic minor coming down: F and G are natural again.
- The same idea works in every minor key. In E minor going up, the 6th (C) and 7th (D) become C sharp and D sharp. In D minor, coming down, B flat and C return, because B flat is in the key signature. Melodic minor: up, raise the 6th and 7th. Down, lower them again. Harmonic minor: the 7th is raised both ways.

**Recap.** Melodic minor going up: the 6th and 7th are raised. | Melodic minor coming down: the 6th and 7th return to the key signature. | Harmonic minor raises only the 7th, up and down.

### G3-U5-L02: Major and minor intervals

**Objective.** Name major and minor intervals above the tonic.

- The 2nd, 3rd, 6th and 7th above the tonic are not perfect. They are major or minor. A minor interval is one semitone smaller than the major interval with the same number. In a major key every one of these intervals is major. Above C, in C major:
- [notation] From C: D is a major 2nd, E a major 3rd, F a perfect 4th, G a perfect 5th, A a major 6th, B a major 7th, C a perfect octave.
- A minor key has a lower 3rd, so the interval from the tonic to the 3rd degree is a minor 3rd. In the harmonic minor, and in the lower form of the melodic minor, the 6th is a minor 6th too. The 2nd is always major.
- [notation] Above C in C minor: minor 3rd (E♭), minor 6th (A♭) and minor 7th (B♭).
- The number comes from counting letter names; the quality comes from the size. The note E♭ above C is still a 3rd, because the letters are C, D, E, but it is a minor 3rd: one semitone smaller than C to E. The pattern is the same in every key, though the notes need different sharps or flats. Look at the notes carefully when you write an interval, and spell the upper note with the letter that gives the right number.
- Count the number from the letters. Then compare with the major scale: the same size is major, a semitone smaller is minor.

**Recap.** Major scales have major 2nds, 3rds, 6ths and 7ths above the tonic. | A minor interval is one semitone smaller than the major one. | The 3rd above the tonic of a minor key is a minor 3rd.

### G3-U2-E1: Upbeat starts

**Objective.** Optional: understand music that begins before the first bar-line.

- This lesson is optional. It helps you read music, but you do not need it to complete Grade 3, and nothing in it is in the Grade 3 assessment.
- Many tunes begin with a note, or a few notes, before the first bar-line. This is called an upbeat, or an anacrusis. The first full bar starts after the upbeat.
- [notation] An upbeat of one crotchet. The first full bar begins after it.
- Because the piece starts part-way through a bar, the last bar is usually shorter than the others. The upbeat and the last bar together add up to one whole bar. A piece in 3/4 that starts with one crotchet upbeat can end with a last bar of two crotchets.
- Whatever the length of the upbeat, group its notes as though they were the end of a bar. The last bar is grouped in the usual way, up to the point where the music stops.
- Upbeat + last bar = one whole bar.

**Recap.** An upbeat is a note or notes before the first bar-line. | The upbeat and the last bar together make one whole bar. | This lesson is optional.

## 4. Conventions the rule set enforces (please confirm each is Grade 3)

- Compound beats are dotted crotchets; the three quavers of a beat are beamed together; no beam crosses a beat line. A whole beat is a dotted crotchet; two whole beats are a dotted minim; a note that starts or ends inside a beat is tied across the beat line, never written as a single note that hides the beat. A tie inside one beat is replaced by a dotted note.
- Rests: a silent bar is a semibreve rest; a silent beat is a dotted-crotchet rest OR a crotchet rest plus a quaver rest (both accepted, Q4); each silent beat has its own rest; three quaver rests for one beat are refused. In 12/8 a single dotted-minim rest may fill beats 1-2 or 3-4 but not beats 2-3 (Q25: an extension of the 4/4 rule; please confirm).
- Partly silent beats (a note followed by a rest inside one beat) are never asked, because the arrangement convention was not verified.
- A two-beat note in the middle (beats 2-3) of a 12/8 bar is never generated, because the reference-book page was only partly legible and the checker's rule for it is lenient.
- Demisemiquavers: never more than four in a row inside a beat, one beam per beat (Q26: the book also allows two groups of four in a crotchet beat).
- Ledger lines: up to four above or below the stave (Q6). Octave signs: 8va above means an octave higher, 8vb below an octave lower.
- Octave transposition: the new clef and the octave move are both required; the key signature is kept.
- Melodic minor: the 6th and 7th are raised going up and lowered coming down; harmonic minor raises the 7th both ways. Spellings such as E♯ and B♯ are required where the letter name demands them.
- Intervals are above the tonic only; the 6th and 7th above a minor tonic are never asked by key (the form would be ambiguous); context melodies never begin with an upbeat (Q5).
- Tonic triads are root position; degrees are numbered (technical names are Grade 4+).

## 7. Known ambiguities and limitations

- Teacher review of every lesson, template, the vocabulary list and the answer tables is outstanding (all content is `draft`).
- Source verification: the reference book's Grade 3 pages (printed pp. 53-77) were consulted for topic order and conventions. The following could not be read with certainty and were not relied on: the two-beat note and two-beat rest conventions in the middle of a 12/8 bar, and the arrangement of rests inside a partly silent beat. They are avoided in questions and flagged above.
- The reference book lists about thirty-four Grade 3 performance words; the course uses the six in the provisional vocabulary (Q2).
- Octave signs are drawn as text, not as a dashed bracket.
- The sounds are synthesised tones. No novice learner test has been run.
- Questions are generated per learner; individual variants are not teacher-reviewed one by one, only the templates and the samples above.
