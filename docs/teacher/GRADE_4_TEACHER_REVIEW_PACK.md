## 2. One representative lesson from each unit

### G4-U1-L02: Double dots

**Objective.** Work out the length of double-dotted notes and rests, and complete bars that hold them.

- A single dot makes a note half as long again: a dotted minim is a minim plus a crotchet. A second dot adds half of what the first dot added, which is a quarter of the note. So a double-dotted minim is a minim, plus a crotchet, plus a quaver.
- [notation] A double-dotted minim lasts 3½ beats; the quaver completes the bar.
- Work in steps. A double-dotted crotchet is a crotchet (1 beat), plus half of it (a quaver, ½ beat), plus a quarter of it (a semiquaver, ¼ beat): 1¾ beats. Its short companion is usually a note that rounds the total up to a whole number of beats, here a semiquaver.
- [notation] 1¾ + ¼ + 1 = 3 beats: a bar of 3/4.
- Rests can be double-dotted too, with exactly the same length as the note of that name. The reference book points out that a second dot sits awkwardly with the usual rule that each beat should start with a new note or rest, so composers often write such rests another way. In this course you work out the length of a double-dotted rest; you are not asked to decide when to write one.
- Each dot adds half of the value before it: note, + ½ note, + ¼ note.

**Recap.** The second dot adds half of what the first dot added. | A double-dotted note is the note plus a half plus a quarter. | A double-dotted rest has the same length as the note of that name.

### G4-U2-L01: Double sharps and double flats

**Objective.** Understand double sharps and double flats, how long they last and how a natural sign cancels them.

- A sharp raises a note by one semitone and a flat lowers it by one. Sometimes a note that is already sharp must go higher still. The double sharp (𝄪) raises a note by two semitones, a whole tone, and the double flat (𝄫) lowers it by two. Each is simply two single signs together.
- [notation] F, F♯ and F𝄪 climb by a semitone each. F𝄪 sounds the same as G.
- A double sign is part of the note's name, so spelling matters. F𝄪 sounds like G, but it is called F because of the letter on the stave. Likewise B𝄫 sounds like A, and E𝄫 like D. Later you will see why a composer chooses the double sign: the letter names have to stay in order.
- Like any accidental, a double sign lasts until the end of the bar, on the same line or space. A natural sign (♮) cancels it, and the note is plain again. At the barline an accidental ends and the key signature takes over again.
- [notation] The natural sign before the last F cancels the double sharp.
- 𝄪 = up two semitones, 𝄫 = down two. A double sign lasts to the barline. A natural sign cancels it. Older music sometimes uses ♮♯ to go from 𝄪 to a single sharp; this course only asks about the natural sign.

**Recap.** The double sharp raises a note two semitones; the double flat lowers it two. | A double sign lasts to the end of the bar; a natural sign cancels it. | Spelling still matters: F𝄪 sounds like G but is written as F.

### G4-U3-L01: B major and G sharp minor

**Objective.** Write the scales and key signatures of B major and G sharp minor, with a double sharp where it is needed.

- Grade 4 adds four new keys, each with five sharps or flats. The two 'sharp' keys are B major and its relative minor, G♯ minor. Five sharps are F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯ and A♯, in that order. For a sharp key the major tonic is a semitone above the last sharp: A♯ is the last, so the key is B major.
- [notation] B major, ascending, written with accidentals instead of a key signature.
- A key signature and an accidental both alter notes, but differently. A key signature at the start of the line alters every note of that letter, in every octave, for the whole piece. An accidental beside a note alters that note, and any later note of the same line or space, only until the barline. When you write a scale without a key signature, every altered note needs its own accidental.
- G♯ minor has the same signature as B major. In the harmonic minor the 7th degree is raised by a semitone. The 7th of G♯ minor is F♯, which is already sharp in the key signature, so raising it makes F𝄪, a double sharp. You cannot write it as G natural, because the scale already has a G♯ as its first note, and every letter name must appear once, in order.
- [notation] G♯ harmonic minor with its key signature: the F𝄪 is written as an accidental.
- Five sharps: B major and G♯ minor. G♯ minor needs F𝄪 for its raised 7th (harmonic) and E♯ and F𝄪 going up in the melodic form; coming down the melodic minor returns to the key signature.

**Recap.** B major and G♯ minor have five sharps: F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ A♯. | G♯ minor's raised 7th is F𝄪; G natural would break the order of letters. | A key signature alters every note of that letter; an accidental alters notes only to the barline.

### G4-U4-L02: Augmented and diminished intervals

**Objective.** Name and write augmented and diminished intervals that arise within a scale.

- Grade 3 needed only three words for quality: major, minor and perfect. Now two more are needed: diminished and augmented. They appear when two notes of a scale are a semitone smaller or larger than the intervals you know, with the same letter names.
- If the top note is lowered by a semitone, or the bottom note raised: major becomes minor, and minor or perfect becomes diminished. If the top note is raised by a semitone, or the bottom note lowered: minor becomes major, and major or perfect becomes augmented. Think of the list diminished – minor – major – augmented, with perfect intervals sitting between diminished and augmented.
- [notation] C major: F up to B is an augmented 4th: a semitone larger than the perfect 4th F to B♭.
- In any major scale the augmented 4th is found between the 4th and 7th degrees, and the diminished 5th between the 7th and the 4th above it. In the harmonic minor, the step from the 6th to the raised 7th is an augmented 2nd, and the 7th up to the 6th is a diminished 7th.
- [notation] A harmonic minor: F up to G♯ is an augmented 2nd.
- Semitone larger: minor → major → augmented, and perfect → augmented. Semitone smaller: major → minor → diminished, and perfect → diminished.

**Recap.** Larger by a semitone: minor → major → augmented; perfect → augmented. | Smaller by a semitone: major → minor → diminished; perfect → diminished. | Augmented 4th and diminished 5th arise between the 4th and 7th degrees; the augmented 2nd arises in the harmonic minor.

### G4-U5-L05: Looking closely at a passage (Grade 4)

**Objective.** Answer questions about intervals, ornaments, directions and instruments in a short passage.

- In the examination, music theory questions often come together in a short piece of music. A single passage may ask about intervals, ornaments, performance directions and the instrument it is written for. Each question uses something you already know; the skill is knowing where to look.
- [notation] A passage for the viola: alto clef, D minor, marked affettuoso, with a trill on the note marked A.
- Method: (1) look at the clef and the key signature: here the alto clef, one flat, D minor. (2) Read the words above the stave: affettuoso, tenderly. (3) Check each sign on the notes: the trill. (4) Think about the instrument: the viola is a string instrument that normally reads the alto clef. Then answer each question on its own, one rule at a time.
- Clef, key, words, signs, instrument. Answer each question with the rule that belongs to it.

**Recap.** Look at the clef, key signature and printed directions first. | Answer each question with the rule that belongs to it. | Use what you know about the instrument named.

## 4. Conventions the rule set enforces (please confirm each is Grade 4)

- A breve is two semibreves and fills a bar of 4/2; a silent bar of 4/2 is a breve rest; in every other time signature a silent bar is a semibreve rest.
- A double dot adds half of what the first dot added. Double-dotted rests are asked only as lengths (the writing convention is unverified).
- Regular time signatures: 2/8, 3/8, 4/8; 2/4, 3/4, 4/4; 2/2, 3/2, 4/2; 6/4, 9/4, 12/4; 6/8, 9/8, 12/8; 6/16, 9/16, 12/16. Simple /16 times (2/16 ...) are excluded (Q7). Compound /4 doubles the 6/8 values and /16 halves them; 4/8 is grouped as 2/4.
- A duplet is two equal notes in the time of three; it fills one compound beat and equals two dotted notes of the same name.
- A double sign lasts to the barline; a natural sign cancels it (Q9, the only cancelling convention asked).
- Enharmonic spelling matters: a spelling-sensitive answer is wrong if only the sound matches. F double sharp is required in G♯ minor harmonic and melodic (ascending); G natural is refused.
- Alto clef: middle C on the middle line, bottom line F, top line G. Key signatures are read and written in the alto clef (Q13); alto-clef octave transposition is required in this course's mapping.
- Intervals: any two notes of one scale within an octave; quality from the major scale of the lower note; augmented and diminished arise diatonically (4th–7th, 7th–4th, harmonic-minor 6th–7th). In minor keys the prompt names the form (Q27).
- Triads: root, 3rd, 5th on I, IV, V; harmonic minor in minor keys; root position means the root is the lowest note; spacing and doubling never change a chord; a doubled note is the same letter an octave apart.
- Chromatic scale: semitones only; every letter used once or twice; no double accidentals; any spelling that follows those rules is accepted.
- Ornaments: trill, turn, upper mordent, lower mordent, acciaccatura (stroked small note), appoggiatura (small note without stroke); only names are asked; ornaments and small notes take no time in the bar.
- Instruments: strings (violin, viola, cello, double bass), woodwind (flute, oboe, clarinet, bassoon), brass (trumpet, horn, trombone, tuba), percussion (timpani, side drum, bass drum, cymbals); order from highest to lowest; normal clefs; only the timpani are pitched.
